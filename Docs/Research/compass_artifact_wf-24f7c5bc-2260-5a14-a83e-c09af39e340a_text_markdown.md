# Legal, Privacy & Ethical Considerations for AI-Powered Email Analytics and Tracking Platforms — A Research Report for an Australian Product Builder

*This is research, not legal advice. Australian legal counsel should review before launch. Current as of August 2026; several regulatory items below post-date mid-2025 and should be independently re-verified.*

## TL;DR
- **Open tracking is now legally radioactive in the EU and increasingly unreliable everywhere.** The EDPB's Guidelines 2/2023 and the CNIL's April 2026 recommendation treat email tracking pixels as "terminal equipment" access under ePrivacy Article 5(3), requiring prior opt-in consent for ordinary marketing open-tracking; Apple Mail Privacy Protection has separately made open rates statistically meaningless by auto-loading pixels for the majority of the market. Build a **privacy-first, aggregate-by-default, click-and-conversion-centric** platform, not an individual-surveillance one.
- **In Australia, your two binding regimes are the Spam Act 2003 (consent/identify/unsubscribe, enforced hard by ACMA) and the Privacy Act 1988 (APPs, plus the new statutory tort and automated-decision-making transparency duties commencing 10 December 2026).** AI engagement scores are "automated decision-making" candidates and, where they profile individuals, personal information.
- **The safe architecture is: collect the minimum, pseudonymise with salted/rotating identifiers, aggregate at cohort level with k-anonymity thresholds, apply short retention, and never let one customer's raw email data train a shared model.** Treat open/click data as personal data by default; obtain granular consent for tracking in the EU; rely on the existing-customer relationship plus legitimate interests carefully elsewhere.

## Key Findings

1. **Email tracking is pervasive and leaky.** The landmark peer-reviewed measurement study (Englehardt, Han & Narayanan, *"I never signed up for this!"*, PoPETs 2018) found 85% of mailing-list emails contained embedded third-party content and 70% contained resources classed as trackers. Verbatim: "about 29% of emails leak the user's email address to at least one third party, and about 19% of senders sent at least one email" with such a leak; "The majority of these leaks (62%) are intentional." This is the scholarly baseline establishing that "anonymous" email tracking usually is not anonymous.

2. **The EU has decisively brought email pixels inside the cookie-consent regime.** EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 (adopted 7 October 2024) confirm Article 5(3) ePrivacy applies to tracking pixels and tracking links because loading them constitutes "gaining access" to information on terminal equipment. The CNIL Recommendation (Délibération 2026-042, adopted 12 March 2026, published 14 April 2026) requires prior consent for ordinary open-tracking, profiling, and fraud-analysis uses, with narrow exemptions for security and basic list-hygiene/deliverability; the Italian Garante is following on a parallel timeline. Compliance deadline for pre-existing addresses: 14 July 2026 (France), late October 2026 (Italy).

3. **Australian law is the priority.** The Spam Act 2003 requires express or inferred consent, sender identification, and a functional unsubscribe honoured within five business days. ACMA is an aggressive enforcer: per the ACMA, "Businesses have paid more than $12 million in spam and telemarketing penalties over the past 18 months," and "in 2024-25 the ACMA issued infringement notices to companies totalling more than $13.5 million" in spam prevention alone. Named penalties include Sportsbet — which per the ACMA (8 Feb 2022) "paid a record infringement notice of just over $2.5 million and has committed to refund around $1.2 million" after messaging "over 37,000 consumers who had tried to unsubscribe" — plus TAB/Tabcorp (a 2024 penalty of "more than $4 million for non-compliant SMS and WhatsApp messages," and a further 2026 action where "TAB pays $2.7m"). The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 introduced a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy (commenced 10 June 2025) and new APP 1.7–1.9 automated-decision-making transparency requirements (commence 10 December 2026).

4. **Whether open/click data is "personal data" is largely settled as YES.** In the EU, the "means reasonably likely to be used" test (Breyer C-582/14; confirmed and refined in EDPS v SRB C-413/23 P, 4 September 2025) makes engagement data tied to an email address personal data in the sender's hands. In Australia, Grubb v Telstra established that IP address and network metadata can be personal information where linkable to an individual, though the AAT's later ruling narrowed this where data is kept genuinely separate.

5. **AI engagement scoring is "profiling" and potentially "automated decision-making."** GDPR Article 22 restricts solely-automated decisions with legal/similarly-significant effects; the CJEU's SCHUFA ruling (C-634/21, Dec 2023) held that scoring driving downstream decisions is caught even when a human nominally signs off. California's finalised CPPA ADMT regulations (OAL-approved 23 September 2025, effective 1 January 2026, ADMT obligations from 1 January 2027) create access/opt-out rights for automated "significant decisions." The **EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)** adds Article 5 prohibitions on manipulative/subliminal techniques (Art 5(1)(a)), exploitation of vulnerabilities (Art 5(1)(b)), and emotion recognition in workplace/education (Art 5(1)(f)) — applicable since 2 February 2025, with fines under Art 99(3) of "up to EUR 35 000 000 or, if the offender is an undertaking, up to 7% of its total worldwide annual turnover."

6. **Privacy-preserving analytics is technically mature but has hard limits at small list sizes.** Differential privacy is deployed by Apple (local DP), Google (RAPPOR, Attribution Reporting API), and the US Census. But local DP noise grows with √n, so at typical marketing list sizes (thousands, not millions) DP destroys utility for fine-grained metrics. Aggregate, k-anonymous cohort reporting is the pragmatic path.

## Details

### 1. Email tracking technologies — how they work, accuracy, and privacy implications

**Tracking pixels (1×1 beacons).** A tracking pixel is a tiny (often 1×1 transparent) image referenced by a unique per-recipient URL. When the mail client fetches the image, the server logs the request — recording open status, IP address (→ approximate geolocation), user-agent (→ client/device), and timestamp. Because the URL is unique per recipient, the "anonymous" image request is de facto tied to a named individual. The EDPB describes a tracking pixel as a hyperlink that "usually fulfils no purpose related to the requested content itself; its sole purpose is to automatically establish a communication by the client to the host of the pixel." That footprint is exactly what makes it legally exposed in the EU.

**Open tracking and why it is now unreliable.**
- *Apple Mail Privacy Protection (MPP).* Launched 20 September 2021 (iOS 15/macOS Monterey), MPP pre-fetches all remote content through Apple's proxy regardless of whether the user opened the message, and hides the real IP. The result is auto-inflated opens. The scale is decisive: per Litmus's Email Client Market Share report (from over 1 billion opens, May 2026), Apple's MPP "now impact[s] roughly 55-60% of all email opens," with Apple Mail alone at 49.79% of opens (Litmus, 2025). In the Rejoiner dataset (sampled from 78M opens), "Apple MPP opens represent over 70% of 2022 total opens." Open rate and click-to-open rate (CTOR) are therefore no longer trustworthy human-engagement signals. ESPs (Mailchimp; Klaviyo's "Apple Privacy open" flag; HubSpot's "adjusted open rate"; Brevo's MPP exclusion) offer filtering, but none can definitively separate human from bot opens.
- *Gmail image proxying.* Google routes remote images through its own proxy servers, normalising IP/user-agent, so opens report Google's infrastructure rather than the user's device.
- *Prefetching and bot/scanner opens.* Security gateways, link scanners, and spam filters fetch pixels and follow links before delivery, generating phantom opens and clicks that inflate metrics and can trigger automation incorrectly.
- *Practical consequence:* Clicks, replies, conversions, and on-site behaviour are the reliable engagement signals; opens are at best a weak directional metric.

**Click tracking (link rewriting/redirects, UTM, link decoration).** Outbound links are rewritten to route through a tracking domain that logs the click then 302-redirects to the destination. UTM parameters (utm_source, utm_medium, utm_campaign) and link decoration append identifiers to attribute downstream behaviour. Academic work (e.g., PURL) shows link decoration is widely abused to exfiltrate email addresses and browser-storage data to third parties. Link rewriting also creates a **security liability**: a naive redirector is an open-redirect vulnerability (see §6).

**Reply tracking.** Detected via unique Reply-To addresses or sub-addressing, In-Reply-To/References headers and Message-ID matching, and threading heuristics. Because replies require deliberate human action, they are a high-quality engagement signal unaffected by MPP.

**Engagement scoring / behavioural profiling.** Platforms aggregate opens, clicks, reply behaviour, recency/frequency, and on-site actions into lead/engagement scores, increasingly using ML/predictive models. This is where analytics becomes **profiling** in the legal sense and, potentially, automated decision-making.

**Server-side vs client-side, CNAME cloaking, fingerprinting.** To evade tracker-blocking, the industry has moved tracking into the first-party context. Peer-reviewed measurement (Demir et al., "Towards Understanding First-Party Cookie Tracking in the Field," 2022) found 76% of top-15,000 sites effectively used such techniques, with CNAME-cloaking usage rising >50% over 2021; Dao et al. found CNAME-based tracking on ~1,762 of Alexa top-300K sites by January 2020, and Dimova et al. found ~9.98% of Tranco top-10K by October 2020. CNAME cloaking aliases a first-party subdomain via DNS to a third-party tracker, defeating third-party protections. Pixel-based tracking also enables fingerprinting via IP + user-agent + timing. These techniques increase legal risk (regulators view them as consent-circumvention) and should be avoided in a privacy-first product.

### 2. Regulations — obligations, scope, enforcement, penalties

**Australia — Spam Act 2003 (Cth).** Governs "commercial electronic messages." Three core duties: (1) **consent** — express, or inferred from an existing relationship/conspicuous publication (Schedule 2); (2) **sender identification**; (3) **functional unsubscribe** honoured within five business days (Spam Regulations 2021). Penalties are per-day, per-message (Schedule 3): sending >50 CEMs without consent in a day = 1,000 penalty units (≈A$313,000 for contraventions after 1 July 2023). Named enforcement targets include Ticketek, The Wine Group, Sportsbet, Kogan (A$310,800), Woolworths, Uber, Latitude Finance (~A$1.55m), Outdoor Supacentre (A$302,500), and TAB/Tabcorp. ACMA's 1 July 2024 Statement of Expectations emphasises consent record-keeping. **Note:** open/click tracking pixels are not directly regulated by the Spam Act — but sending the tracked message at all requires Spam Act compliance.

**Australia — Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and 2024 reforms.** Thirteen Australian Privacy Principles bind "APP entities": notably APP 3 (collection), APP 5 (notification), APP 6 (use/disclosure), APP 7 (direct marketing), APP 8 (cross-border disclosure), APP 11 (security). The **Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024** (Royal Assent 10 December 2024) introduced a **statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy** (intrusion upon seclusion or misuse of information; commenced 10 June 2025); **automated decision-making transparency** via new APP 1.7–1.9 requiring privacy policies to disclose substantially-automated decisions that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual's rights or interests (commence 10 December 2026, two-year grace); criminal doxxing offences; and enhanced OAIC powers/compliance notices. This is a **disclosure** regime, not an EU-style prohibition. Direct-marketing breaches of APP 7 now carry civil penalty exposure. A small-business exemption exists under the current Act, but reform is ongoing.

**GDPR (EU/EEA).** Processing engagement data requires a lawful basis (Art 6): typically **consent** (Art 6(1)(a)) or **legitimate interests** (Art 6(1)(f)). But ePrivacy consent for the pixel is a prerequisite (below), and marketing profiling is subject to the Art 21 right to object. **Article 22** restricts solely-automated decisions with legal/similarly-significant effects; per EDPB 2018 guidelines and SCHUFA (C-634/21), scores driving significant downstream decisions are caught; ordinary marketing profiling usually is not "significant," but health/financial-vulnerability targeting can be. Transparency: Arts 13/14. **DPIAs** (Art 35) are required for systematic large-scale profiling. Data minimisation and purpose limitation (Art 5) are binding. Fines up to €20m or 4% of global turnover.

**ePrivacy Directive Art 5(3) and email pixels.** EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 (adopted 7 October 2024) confirm Art 5(3) covers pixels, tracking links, fingerprinting, and some IP-based tracking, on four criteria (information; terminal equipment; public communications network; gaining access or storage — either suffices; "information" includes non-personal data). The CNIL Recommendation (Délibération 2026-042) grounds email-pixel consent in Article 82 of the French Data Protection Act (France's Art 5(3) transposition): consent is required for open-rate measurement, profiling, and fraud analysis; exemptions are narrow (authentication/security; deliverability-based list hygiene for user-requested/transactional mail only). Consent must be collected at address-collection time, withdrawal made easy, and a contract delegating consent-collection to a third-party platform is insufficient proof. The Bavarian DPA (BayLDA) separately ruled a German company's use of US-based Mailchimp unlawful post-Schrems II for failure to assess supplementary transfer measures (no fine, as use had ceased and data was low-sensitivity) — a data-transfer, not pixel, decision, but relevant to vendor choice.

**CAN-SPAM (US) and FTC.** Opt-out (not opt-in) regime: accurate headers, non-deceptive subject lines, valid physical postal address, functional opt-out honoured within 10 business days. No B2B exemption. Penalties up to $53,088 per email (inflation-adjusted). Enforcement is rare but escalating: per DOJ/FTC (30 Aug 2024), Verkada agreed to "a $2.95 million civil penalty" — "the largest penalty obtained by the FTC for a CAN-SPAM violation" — for "sending more than 30 million commercial emails over a three-year period" without a working unsubscribe or valid postal address; Experian Consumer Services settled for $650,000 (August 2023) over a broken opt-out on "transactional-looking" promotional email.

**CCPA/CPRA (California).** Broad "sale"/"sharing" definitions capture ad-tech data sharing; consumers have a right to opt out of sale/sharing, plus sensitive-personal-information limits. The **finalised CPPA regulations** (OAL-approved 23 September 2025; effective 1 January 2026; ADMT obligations from 1 January 2027; risk assessments and cybersecurity audits phased through 2030) impose notice, access, and opt-out rights for ADMT used in "significant decisions." **Global Privacy Control (GPC) must be honoured** as a valid opt-out: the California AG's Sephora settlement (announced 24 August 2022) required Sephora to pay "$1.2 million in penalties" for, among other things, failing to "Treat signals from 'user-enabled global privacy controls' the same as requests to opt out of the sale of personal information." Subsequent CPPA enforcement referencing GPC includes Honda (announced 12 March 2025, $632,500) and Todd Snyder (announced 6 May 2025, $345,178, where "GPC signals were not honored during [a 40-day] outage" and the company had "deferred to third-party privacy management tools without knowing their limitations"). In September 2025 the CPPA joined the California, Colorado, and Connecticut AGs in an investigative sweep specifically into GPC non-compliance.

**EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).** Article 5(1)(a) prohibits AI deploying "subliminal techniques beyond a person's consciousness or purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques" that materially distort behaviour and cause significant harm; Art 5(1)(b) prohibits exploiting vulnerabilities due to "age, disability or a specific social or economic situation"; Art 5(1)(f) prohibits emotion inference "in the areas of workplace and education institutions." Per the Commission's February 2025 Guidelines, only biometric-data-based emotion inference is caught — "sentiment analysis conducted on written words is not caught" — and the workplace/education confinement means ordinary consumer email analytics is likely outside Art 5(1)(f). But the Guidelines expressly left the scope of "targeted advertising" unconfirmed, and Art 5(1)(a)/(b) could bite AI-personalised dark patterns exploiting vulnerable consumers. Art 5 prohibitions have applied since 2 February 2025; breach exposes an undertaking to "up to EUR 35 000 000 or... up to 7% of its total worldwide annual turnover" (Art 99(3)). No enforcement cases exist yet.

**Also relevant:** UK GDPR + PECR (ICO guidance mirrors the EU pixel-as-tracking analysis); Canada's CASL (opt-in by default, narrow time-limited implied consent, 10-business-day unsubscribe, penalties up to CAD $1m individuals / $10m businesses; CRTC enforcement including the CAD $1.1m Compu-Finder notice, later set at $200,000, and a $75,000 penalty against Scott William Brewer for 670,000+ emails). The **EDPB "consent or pay" Opinion 08/2024** (adopted 17 April 2024) found that large online platforms will "in most cases... not be possible" to obtain valid consent if users face only a binary "consent-or-pay" choice, and that "personal data cannot be considered as a tradeable commodity."

### 3. Legal reasoning — what can be collected, on what basis, and what must be de-identified

**What is personal data / personal information?** Open/click events tied to a recipient email address or unique tracking ID are personal data in the EU (Breyer relative-identifiability test) and personal information in Australia (Grubb — metadata can be personal information where linkable to an individual). Aggregate, non-re-identifiable cohort statistics are not.

**Consent vs legitimate interests vs contractual necessity.**
- *EU pixel/open-tracking:* **prior opt-in consent** (ePrivacy Art 5(3)) — legitimate interests cannot cure the ePrivacy consent gap. Separate from consent to receive the email itself.
- *EU processing of resulting data:* consent or legitimate interests (Art 6), but consent for the terminal-equipment access remains the gating requirement.
- *EU automated significant decisions:* explicit consent, contractual necessity, or law (Art 22(2)) — legitimate interests does **not** suffice.
- *Australia:* Spam Act consent to send; APPs govern the resulting personal information; direct marketing under APP 7.
- *US (CAN-SPAM):* no consent needed to send, but opt-out must work; CCPA opt-out of sale/sharing and GPC.

**Anonymisation vs pseudonymisation.** Pseudonymised data (salted-hash IDs with a separately-held key) remains personal data for the party holding the key, but — per **EDPS v SRB (C-413/23 P, 4 Sept 2025)** — may be anonymous *in the hands of a recipient* who lacks reasonable means to re-identify. Anonymisation (irreversible, no reasonable re-identification) removes data from GDPR scope entirely (Art 29 WP Opinion 05/2014). The distinction matters for reporting exports and vendor sharing.

**When does a score become "profiling"?** Any automated evaluation of personal aspects (interests, behaviour, reliability) is profiling (GDPR Art 4(4)). It becomes **Article 22 automated decision-making** only when solely automated AND producing legal/similarly-significant effects — e.g., excluding someone from an offer, dynamic pricing, or (edge case) inferring vulnerability. Ordinary send-time or content optimisation usually is not "significant," but you must still be transparent and honour objection rights.

### 4. Privacy-first architecture

**Contrast.**
- *Traditional (avoid):* Person → per-recipient Email Open Event (unique pixel + IP + UA + timestamp) → persistent Individual Profile → per-person engagement score → individual targeting/surveillance.
- *Privacy-first (build):* Campaign/Cohort → Aggregate Engagement Events (counted at cohort level, k-anonymised) → Insights (cohort open/click distributions, A/B lift, send-time curves, deliverability) with no durable individual-level behavioural profile.

**Data-flow (described).** (1) Send layer: generate a **per-send, salted, rotating pseudonym** (not a stable per-person ID) so events cannot be trivially linked across campaigns. (2) Edge collection: an ingestion endpoint truncates IP (e.g., zero the last octet of IPv4 / last 80 bits of IPv6) at the edge, discards user-agent beyond coarse client class, and does not persist raw IP. (3) Aggregation service: events roll up into cohort counters; only cohorts meeting a **k-anonymity threshold (e.g., k ≥ 50, higher for sensitive segments)** are reportable. (4) Storage: separate the identifier store (email ↔ salt key, tightly access-controlled, short TTL) from the analytics store (pseudonymous events, then aggregates). (5) Retention automation: raw events purged fast (30–90 days), aggregates retained longer.

**Schema sketch.** `contacts`(id, email_hash, consent_state, consent_timestamp, jurisdiction) — restricted. `send_pseudonyms`(pseudonym, campaign_id, salt_epoch, expiry) — rotates. `events_raw`(pseudonym, event_type, coarse_geo, ts) — short retention. `cohort_metrics`(campaign_id, cohort_key, opens_est, clicks, replies, conversions, n) — n must exceed k before display.

**Retention schedule (suggested).** Raw event logs: 30–90 days. Pseudonym maps: rotate per campaign, delete after the attribution window. Cohort aggregates: 12–24 months. Consent records: life of relationship + limitation period (keep robust proof, given ACMA's record-keeping expectations).

**Preserving useful analytics without individual surveillance.** Cohort-level engagement distributions; A/B testing on aggregate lift; send-time optimisation from aggregate open-time curves (not per-person); deliverability monitoring (bounces, spam-complaint rates, domain reputation), which is legitimate list-hygiene and largely within CNIL's deliverability carve-out.

### 5. Technical depth — privacy-preserving measurement

**Anonymous/aggregate analytics.** Cookieless, server-aggregated measurement; Google's **Privacy Sandbox Attribution Reporting API** produces summary reports from encrypted per-user contributions, adding discrete-Laplace noise scaled to an L1 contribution budget and enforcing single-query budgeting inside a trusted execution environment (a formal DP guarantee shown by Google Research and academic analysis of ARA/PAA). Apple Private Relay / MPP proxying means IP-based signals are unreliable for Apple users — reinforcing aggregate design. Approaches analogous to **Prio** (secret-shared aggregation) compute sums without any server seeing individual values.

**Differential privacy.** Formal ε-DP bounds how much any one record changes the output distribution; smaller ε = more privacy. **Local DP** (RAPPOR in Chrome; Apple; Microsoft Windows telemetry) randomises on-device before transmission — but noise standard deviation grows ∝ √(report count), and Apple's real-world per-datum budget was measured at ε=1–2 while the *system* budget reached as high as 16 (Tang et al.), illustrating the theory-vs-deployment gap. **Central DP** (US Census; Uber SQL) adds less noise but requires trusting a central aggregator with raw data. **Applicability to email:** DP works at Apple/Google population scale; at typical marketing list sizes (a few thousand recipients), the √n noise swamps the signal, so DP is largely **impractical for small-list fine-grained metrics**. Use it only for large aggregate cross-campaign reporting, if at all; otherwise rely on k-anonymity + suppression.

**Data minimisation.** Hash+truncate IP; avoid storing full user-agent; prefer event-count aggregates over event-level logs; don't collect what you won't use.

**Consent management.** CMPs with granular, purpose-specific consent; **double opt-in** for EU/Canada lists; durable consent records with timestamp and proof (contractual delegation is insufficient per CNIL). Beware IAB TCF: the Belgian DPA (APD) fined IAB Europe €250,000 (2 Feb 2022), found TC Strings to be personal data and IAB a (joint) controller; the Brussels Market Court (14 May 2025) largely upheld this after the CJEU's C-604/22 ruling — so TCF membership does not equal compliance. Honour **GPC** as an opt-out signal. Approach "consent-or-pay" with caution given EDPB Opinion 08/2024.

### 6. Best-practice recommendations

**Structured risk assessment (likelihood × impact).** High-likelihood/high-impact: EU pixel-consent non-compliance; ACMA unsubscribe/consent breach; open-redirect abuse of your link-rewriter; training shared models on customer data. Medium: deliverability/reputation damage from bot-inflated metrics driving bad automation; DSAR non-fulfilment. Build a **DPIA/PIA** triggered by: any new profiling/scoring feature, any cross-border transfer, any special-category inference, any ADMT with significant effects. Template fields: purpose, lawful basis, data categories, necessity/proportionality, risks to individuals, mitigations, residual risk, sign-off.

**Technical controls.** Encryption in transit/at rest; least-privilege access control and audit logging; pseudonymisation pipeline with separated key store; automated retention/purge; DSAR/access-request tooling keyed to email_hash; **data residency** — keep Australian and EU data in-region and vet sub-processors (the Mailchimp/BayLDA lesson: run transfer risk assessments). **Secure link-rewriting:** never redirect to a URL supplied in an unvalidated parameter — use a server-side allowlist/mapping of destination IDs (OWASP Unvalidated Redirects & Forwards guidance) to prevent your tracking domain becoming a phishing open-redirect and getting blocklisted. Authenticate with SPF/DKIM/DMARC, warm sending IPs/domains, and monitor blocklists so tracking infrastructure is not flagged.

**Product recommendations.** Default tracking **off** in the EU until consent is captured; expose opens as a clearly-labelled unreliable/estimated metric post-MPP and surface **clicks, replies, conversions, and on-site actions** as primary KPIs. Build cohort A/B and send-time tools, not per-person surveillance dashboards. Market "privacy-first analytics" honestly. **On AI model training:** customer email content and recipient behavioural data should **not** train shared/cross-customer models without explicit, specific consent and strong de-identification — default to per-tenant models or aggregate-only features; contaminating a shared model with one customer's personal data is a serious purpose-limitation and confidentiality risk.

**Ethics beyond compliance.** Avoid dark patterns and manipulative defaults; respect the power asymmetry between sender and recipient; treat dignity as a design constraint. **Inferring emotional or health states from engagement behaviour is a bright line** — high manipulation risk, likely special-category data if health-related, potentially within EU AI Act Art 5(1)(a)/(b), and reputationally toxic even where technically legal. Adopt an internal ethics review and align with professional/industry codes (e.g., ADMA in Australia).

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Before any launch (now):**
- Engage Australian privacy counsel; map data flows and complete a PIA.
- Make Spam Act basics bulletproof: consent capture with proof, sender ID, five-business-day unsubscribe. This is your highest-frequency enforcement risk.
- Default to aggregate/cohort analytics; ship k-anonymity thresholds (start k≥50) and IP truncation from day one.
- Secure your link-rewriter against open redirects (allowlist mapping).

**Stage 2 — For EU/global expansion:**
- Turn open-tracking **off by default** for EU recipients; capture granular ePrivacy consent at signup; run the CNIL-style information-and-objection process for legacy addresses before 14 July 2026 (France) / late October 2026 (Italy).
- Stand up DSAR tooling and a consent record store; honour GPC.
- Run transfer risk assessments for any non-EU/non-AU sub-processor.

**Stage 3 — For AI features:**
- Classify each score: Is it profiling? Is it a "significant" automated decision (GDPR Art 22 / CPPA ADMT / APP 1.7–1.9)? If significant, add human-review, explanation, and opt-out.
- Prepare APP 1.7–1.9 automated-decision disclosures ahead of 10 December 2026.
- Never train shared models on customer personal data without explicit consent; screen any inference feature against EU AI Act Art 5.

**Benchmarks that change the plan:** If Apple Mail opens exceed ~50% of your measured opens (they now typically do), stop using opens in automation entirely. If any cohort falls below your k-threshold, suppress it. If a feature infers health/financial vulnerability, halt and escalate to ethics/legal review.

- **Post-mid-2025 items to re-verify:** CNIL/Garante email-pixel deadlines (July/Oct 2026); CPPA ADMT phase-in dates; Australian APP 1.7–1.9 commencement and OAIC guidance (consultation closed mid-2026); EDPS v SRB downstream guidance; EU AI Act applicability to marketing (and any "Digital Omnibus" deferral of high-risk deadlines, which does *not* affect the Art 5 prohibitions in force since 2 Feb 2025); the Tractor Supply CPPA penalty amount (reported as the CPPA's largest to date but not confirmed here).
- **Unsettled/conflicting law:** whether legitimate interests can ever ground EU open-tracking (regulators say no for the pixel itself); the exact reach of Art 22 to marketing scores; Grubb's narrowing on appeal means the Australian "personal information" status of raw network data is context-dependent; DP at small list sizes is widely regarded as impractical but there is no bright-line list-size rule; the EU AI Act's reach into targeted advertising was expressly left open by the Commission's Guidelines.
- **Vendor/industry sources** (ESP blogs, CMP vendors, standards-body explainers) were used only for factual technical/market claims and are clearly lower-tier than the peer-reviewed and regulator sources.
